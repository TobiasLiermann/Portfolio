    Project 01 Robotics / Embedded HS Düsseldorf · 2026
    Sekuria
    Cobra RS
    Articulated robot · 5 axes + gripper
    Built 1980s Made in Germany Owner HSD Rebuilt 2026

    An industrial robot arm from the 1980s whose control system had been lost. During a four-week internship at Hochschule Düsseldorf, I rebuilt the electronics and software from scratch. Without plans or original software, I had to find out how the arm is built, choose the electronics and program the control system again from the ground up. In the end, the arm detected cubes with a ToF sensor, picked them up and stacked them.

    Starting point

    The Cobra RS is a robot arm made by Sekuria in the 1980s. It belongs to Hochschule Düsseldorf (HSD), a university of applied sciences. The mechanics were complete, and the six Philips ID35 steppers were still installed and wired. The control system, however, was missing completely: no drivers, no controller and no original documentation. The arm could not move.

    I did the four-week internship as part of my vocational training at the Lore-Lorentz-Schule. I was supervised by Prof. Ralf Beck and Mario Meilchen in the Department of Electrical Engineering and Information Technology. I had chosen the project together with them during an earlier visit to the HSD. My task was to choose the hardware, wire it and write the firmware myself, until the arm moved again and could be controlled through different interfaces. Because there was no documentation left, I had to work out and measure a lot of things on the arm itself.

    Understanding the mechanics

    Before I could write a single line of kinematics, I had to find out how the arm works mechanically. It has six steppers but only five axes of motion: the sixth motor closes the gripper, and a spring opens it again.

    Belt drive and decoupled axes

    The motors are not in the joints but at the bottom of the arm. The outer joints are driven by toothed belts that run along the arm segments. Because of this belt layout, the axes are mechanically decoupled: every angle is measured against the horizontal and not relative to the previous segment. When the shoulder moves, the hand keeps its orientation.

    How old the belts were became clear in the second-to-last week: the shoulder belt crumbled, and the others were close to it. We measured the profile (T5), and when the replacement arrived the following Monday, I took the arm apart and replaced the belts.

    Differential wrist

    The pitch and roll of the hand are produced by both wrist motors together through a bevel gear differential. If both turn in the same direction, the result is pure pitch. If they turn in opposite directions, the result is pure roll. So the firmware first calculates the two motor angles from the desired pitch and roll:

    θM4 = Pitch + Roll
    θM5 = Pitch − Roll Signs determined by testing

    I found the signs by testing on the arm, because they depend on how the motors are mounted and wired. There is no free yaw for the hand. Yaw only comes from rotating the base.

    Hardware

    Drivers6× TMC2209, STEP/DIR + UART
    MotorsPhilips ID35, 12 V, 7.5°/step (48 steps/rev), bipolar
    Power supply18 V
    Microstepping16 → 768 microsteps/rev
    Homingoptical reflective sensor (CNY70/TCRT5000 type) per joint
    Environment sensorVL53L1X ToF sensor on the hand, I²C

    The controller is an ESP32-P4-ETH. Wired Ethernet instead of Wi-Fi was a request from the university. The six TMC2209 drivers sit on a stepper board that was actually made for the Odroid M1. It distributes power to all drivers, so I did not have to connect each driver separately, and the wiring stayed clear. Over UART, the motor current and microstepping can be set in software.

    The ID35 are 6-wire motors. They were already wired as bipolar motors using the four outer wires, so I could connect them directly to the TMC2209. Everything is powered with 18 V instead of the motors' rated 12 V. The higher voltage gives more power and therefore smoother movements.

    For the electronics, I designed a mounting frame in Fusion 360 and 3D printed it. It is screwed onto the base plate of the arm. The controller, stepper board and breadboard each have a fixed place in it, so everything sits firmly and nothing lies loose on the table.

    Key decisions

    Step generation

    A stepper only moves when it gets one pulse for every step. With 16 microsteps, that is 768 pulses per motor revolution, and the time between them has to be even, otherwise the motor runs roughly. The FastAccelStepper library does not create these pulses in software but with the RMT module of the ESP32. These are separate hardware units that send out the pulses on their own while the processor does other work.

    The plan was to drive all six motors this way. But the error messages showed that only four RMT channels were available to me on the ESP32-P4. So I split the motors: motors 1 to 3 and the gripper motor 6 run hardware-timed through FastAccelStepper. The two wrist motors 4 and 5 are driven by AccelStepper, which creates the pulses in software in its own FreeRTOS task. The wrist does not need to move or react quickly, so it was the best place to do without the hardware. The downside is visible on the arm: the wrist axes move more slowly than the others. So that the rest of the firmware does not need to know which library is behind a motor, I wrapped both in shared functions.

    The ESP32-P4 has two cores. The web server runs on core 0 and the motion control on core 1. This way, a request to the web interface does not slow down a running movement, and a movement does not block the web server.

    Driver tuning

    The TMC2209 has two operating modes. StealthChop is quiet but gives less torque at higher speeds. SpreadCycle is louder and stronger. StealthChop was enough on almost all axes. Only the shoulder, motor 2, lost steps when the rest of the arm was stretched forward, because it then holds the most weight. For this axis, the driver therefore switches to SpreadCycle above a certain speed.

    I set the running current to 240 mA, the value from the ID35 datasheet. In continuous operation, the motors got warm. That is why the driver lowers the current to about 30 % when the motor is standing still.

    StallGuard only on the gripper

    The TMC2209 can detect with StallGuard when a motor is blocked, without an extra sensor. The idea was to use this to detect collisions on the arm axes. In practice, though, one of the motors kept triggering, almost at random. The problem is that every axis needs a different amount of force depending on the pose. A threshold that works in one position is too sensitive or not sensitive enough in the next. Calibrating this for every axis and every pose would have been too much work for the internship. So I switched StallGuard off on the arm axes.

    I kept StallGuard on the gripper. There it stops the motor as soon as the resistance from the gripped cube becomes too high. I found the threshold by first closing the gripper without StallGuard and reading the measured values while it closed.

    Features

    Homing and calibration

    After switching on, a stepper does not know where it is. That is why each joint has an optical reflective sensor that looks at a belt pulley with a white stripe. During homing, axes 1 to 6 move one after another to the edge between black and white: first fast, then a little back, and then a second time slowly, which makes the reference point more accurate. The pull-up resistors needed for this were originally on the old connection board, so I added 10 kΩ pull-ups to 3.3 V on the breadboard.

    I measured the angle at the reference point for the shoulder and elbow with a spirit level app: 33.5° above the horizontal. At first I had the sign wrong. I noticed because the arm moved in the wrong direction.

    Kinematics

    I derived and programmed the forward kinematics myself. I solved the inverse kinematics with the help of AI. For the position, a 3R kinematic chain of base, shoulder and elbow is enough. The orientation of the hand is added through the differential mixing. The decoupled axes make the calculation easier: the direction of an arm segment comes directly from its own angle and not from the sum of all previous angles.

    Two more points came up while driving the arm. So that the arm does not swing out wide or move in a jerky path, the axis with the longest way moves at full speed and the others proportionally slower, so that all of them arrive at the same time. The firmware also checks all angles against the axis limits before any motor moves. An unreachable point is skipped instead of letting the axes move out of control.

    Web interface

    A web server with a control page runs on the ESP32. With it, I give the arm a table of points that it moves to one after another, move single axes directly and see the axis angles live. When moving an axis directly, the page sends a heartbeat every 200 ms. If it is missing for 500 ms, the motor stops. Behind the page is a REST API that other programs can use as well. I created the web server and the control page with the help of AI, because I have little experience with web development so far and wanted to focus on the robotics. I implemented myself how the API works together with the functions of the robot control code.

    ROS2 and digital twin

    I used ROS2 for the 3D model and the cube detection. The programs run on a laptop and get their data through the same REST API as the web interface. This was simpler than micro-ROS on the ESP32. I designed the 3D model myself in Fusion 360, exported the single parts as COLLADA (.dae) so that rviz2 shows the colours, and put them back together into a moving arm in a URDF file. It moves live together with the real arm. For this, the angles are converted, because the firmware calculates against the horizontal and the model relative to the previous segment.

    Cube detection with the ToF sensor

    A VL53L1X sits on the gripper in a mount I printed myself. It is a time-of-flight sensor that measures distances by the travel time of light. I chose it because I had one at home and had already experimented with it out of interest. Its field of view can be split into 4×4 zones. The arm moves to four points above the table and takes a depth image with 16 values at each of them.

    Even the empty, flat table looked like a bowl in these images, because the outer zones look at the table at an angle. Instead of correcting this with trigonometry, which works badly because the viewing angles of the zones are not precise, the arm first records a reference of the empty table and subtracts it during the scan. What is left is only what sticks out above the table. From these zones, a ROS2 program calculates the position of the cube, and then the arm moves there, grips the cube and stacks it. Instead of the black cubes I used at first, I switched to white ones, because they reflect the sensor's light better.

    Result

    In the end, the arm could be controlled through the web interface, and a ROS2 program on the laptop took over the cube search. With the ToF sensor on the hand, the arm scans the table, detects cubes, determines their position, picks them up and stacks them. With four cubes on the table, it usually found three in the first run and sometimes the fourth in a second run.

    What I learned

    The most important thing I learned is that measuring and testing on the real arm is more reliable than any amount of thinking it through. I assumed that only the wrist was decoupled, thought the upper arm was longer than the forearm and had the sign of the home angle wrong. Each time, only testing on the arm showed the mistake. It was similar with the ToF sensor: a reference image of the empty table was simpler and more reliable than calculating the distortion. Calibrating is often easier than calculating.

    I learned how important an emergency stop is from the mistake in the tool length. Because of the wrong sign, the gripper moved 148 mm lower than planned and straight into the table. Luckily, an emergency stop button was connected to the arm.

    In my next project, I would define more clearly at the start what the goal is and what each interface should be able to do. Direct control of single axes was only added shortly before the end of the internship. When I added it, the new buttons blocked themselves at first: "motor running" and "program running" were one shared state, and the interface stopped the movement again after half a second. Only two separate states solved this.

    For my studies, I take away how much I am interested in mechanical design. I actually knew that already, but the internship showed it to me again, with the mounting frame, the sensor mount and the 3D model. Here I understood belts, gear ratios and a bevel gear differential on a real machine instead of only on paper.
