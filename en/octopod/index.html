    Project 02 Robotics / Mechanical design Personal project · since 2025
    Self-built
    Octopod
    Walking robot · 8 legs · 24 servos
    Version V0.2 Material PETG Controller ESP32-S3 Start 2025

    An eight-legged walking robot that I designed, printed and wired completely by myself. Each leg has three servos, 24 in total. There are no building instructions for it: I designed all printed parts myself and kept improving them until everything fit together. The current version V0.2 stands on its own legs without the battery. The big goal is to make it climb a staircase one day.

    Starting point

    It started with a kit for a walking robot, the Maker Kit Bionic by fischertechnik, which I found interesting. But I did not want to buy it for 170 €. Instead, I thought about whether I could build something similar myself for less money and make it better at the same time.

    A brainstorming session turned into a wish list: eight legs instead of four, so an octopod, with three servos per leg. On top of that, a gimbal made of two motors that swivels a time-of-flight sensor and an ultrasonic sensor. The ultrasonic sensor should also detect obstacles made of glass. Everything was to be controlled by a cheap ESP32.

    With the gimbal, I wanted to scan the surroundings and create a rough 2D map. At first I planned to use a VL53L0X for this. Then I got to know the VL53L1X, which delivers more data. Today I would choose a VL53L8CX.

    Choosing parts

    Because saving money was one of the main reasons for the project, I used Google and AI to research which parts are cheap on platforms like AliExpress and still well documented.

    ControllerESP32-S3
    Servos16× MG90S (hip and lower leg), 8× MG996R (upper leg)
    Servo drivers2× PCA9685PW, I²C
    Power supply3S LiPo, 5200 mAh, ~11.1 V → step-down to 5 V
    Protectionautomotive fuse 15 A
    Current measurementINA226 with shunt
    Sensors2× VL53L1X (ToF), HC-SR04 (ultrasonic), MPU-9250 (IMU)

    First prototype V0.1

    Before the eight-legged version, I tested a lighter version with four legs and two servos per leg. I wanted to try out the servos, the driver boards and how stable the prints are.

    The prototype quickly showed what does not work. With four legs it was far from stable enough, and the electronic parts slid out of the recesses I had put them in. I took two things from this: four legs are not enough, and parts are better held with pins than with recesses.

    Design and printing

    I started with CAD in Fusion 360 at school and then kept teaching myself. The Octopod was the first project where I really used it. The base plate has eight connection points for the legs. I first drew the modules on it as placeholders.

    I designed some parts with triangular struts so that they stay stable but weigh little. The lower leg is about 18 cm long. Next time I would build even more parts this way so that the Octopod gets lighter overall.

    I printed everything on an Ender 3 V2 that I was given second-hand because it only half worked. I repaired the broken nozzle on the extruder and levelled the print bed again. After that, it printed PETG without problems. I chose PETG because it is strong and cheap and gives off relatively few fumes while printing.

    Building V0.2

    I call the current version V0.2 and still a prototype, because a lot more will be added in the final version. I designed and printed every part myself and reworked it as often as needed until everything fit together. Without the weight of the battery, the Octopod stands if it has enough grip, for example on a carpet.

    The base plate is 40 cm long and about 20 cm wide. It consists of a front and a back half, held together by small screwed connection parts. Each leg has three parts with one servo each: an MG90S turns the hip (coxa) forward and backward, a stronger MG996R lifts and lowers the upper leg (femur), because that is where most of the weight hangs, and another MG90S bends the lower leg (tibia).

    Electrics

    The power comes from a 3S LiPo battery with 5200 mAh and about 11.1 V. The positive lead first goes through an automotive fuse that blows above 15 A and protects against short circuits. After that comes a shunt. The INA226 measures the voltage drop across it and calculates current and voltage from it. A step-down module then lowers the voltage to 5 V, and from there it is distributed to the two servo drivers and the ESP32.

    On the breadboard, I use one of the two plus/minus rails to distribute power to the servo drivers and sensors. The other one carries the I²C lines SCL and SDA, because the two servo drivers, the INA226 and the MPU-9250 IMU all talk to the ESP32 over I²C. The servos run directly on the 5 V from the step-down module.

    Problems and improvements

    As soon as I stood the Octopod up, with and without the battery, the joint brackets bent under the load. I had underestimated the force acting there when I designed them. The connections gave way so much that the MG996R could not really do its job: the connections would probably have broken before the servo reached its limit.

    I have already improved part of this. At first, the screws were screwed directly into the PETG. Now they go all the way through the PETG and are secured with a nut on the other side. Nothing comes loose anymore, and the connections are much more stable. Still, the servo horns bend a lot, and the gears of the MG90S give way. The real problem is that the servos themselves form the joint axis and carry the whole load.

    For the next version, I am therefore planning ball bearings parallel to the servos, above or below the joint, to guide and support the connection. The servo should then only drive the joint. I am also thinking about using an MG996R on every axis instead of the weaker MG90S.

    So far, the software can move the legs and read the INA226. The main difficulty was that almost every servo is mounted facing a different direction. I solved this with a table that stores the direction of rotation for each servo as a sign.

    Current status and next steps

    The Octopod is an ongoing project. In July 2026, I made the first walking attempts, once with the old connections and once with screws and nuts. It did not get further than about 5 cm. So first it has to learn to walk properly. After that, it should scan its surroundings with the ToF and ultrasonic sensors, detect obstacles, doors and stairs, and create a map of the room. I want to control it through a website, change parameters like the speed and see the sensor data. A small OLED display should show the battery level. With the IMU and the INA226, I want to calibrate the legs when they have shifted, and a separate 6 V circuit for the servos should give them more power.

    The big goal is for the Octopod to climb a staircase. Maybe it will not work, but I will try.

    What I learned

    The most important lesson from V0.2 is that I have to estimate beforehand how much load a part has to carry, and then test whether it can handle it. When I designed the joint brackets, I did not think about the load at all. Only the standing robot showed that they give way.

    For connections in 3D-printed parts, a simple solution worked best: a screw all the way through the part and a nut on the other side. It holds firmly and is easier than melted-in threaded inserts.

    Next time, I would also make more use of structures. I already used triangles for the lower leg, but the base plate is still solid. With a honeycomb pattern of holes, I would probably have saved quite a lot of weight there.
